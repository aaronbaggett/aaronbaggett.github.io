For any chapter that spans two weeks, the reading is split at a natural midpoint, and the chapter’s learning objectives are divided to match: the first week targets the lower-order objectives (Remember, Understand), and the second week targets the higher-order objectives (Apply, Analyze, Evaluate). Single-week chapters address all five objectives together. Fridays remain the hybrid/flex day for that week’s online individual assignment; no class meets Monday, October 5 (campus holiday).

Course Modules and Learning Objectives

  1. Two Minds, One Question: The Formative Lives of Freud and Lewis (Ch. 1)

    1. Summarize the intellectual influences of both Freud and Lewis that led each man to reject his childhood faith and embrace atheism.
    2. Differentiate the developmental trajectories of Freud and Lewis despite their similar early experiences, identifying the points at which their paths diverged.
    3. Compare the chapter’s portrayal of loss and father-child conflict with Psalm 27:10, assessing how a biblical view of fatherhood interprets Freud’s and Lewis’s experiences differently than a purely psychoanalytic account.

  2. The Big Questions: Creator and Conscience as Tests of Worldview (Chs. 2–3)

    1. Analyze the evidentiary basis Freud and Lewis each offer for their claims about a Creator, distinguishing appeals to evidence from appeals to emotion or personal history (e.g., Freud’s experience of antisemitism).
    2. Compare Lewis’s reasoning toward “an Intelligence beyond the universe” with Romans 1:20, evaluating points of agreement and difference.
    3. Differentiate between Freud’s superego-based explanation and Lewis’s alternative explanation for the origin and function of conscience.
    4. Analyze the chapter’s central question–whether conscience reflects a universal moral law or an evolved psychological adaptation–by weighing the evidence each position offers.

  3. Crossing Over: Conversion, Credibility, and the Great Transition (Ch. 4)

    1. Examine the criteria Nicholi uses to distinguish a genuine worldview conversion from psychological pathology.
    2. Analyze the relative contributions of intellect and emotion to worldview change, using Lewis’s own account of his transition as the primary case study.
    3. Compare the module’s account of conversion with 2 Corinthians 5:17, assessing what a biblical doctrine of transformation adds to, or challenges in, a psychological account of change.

  4. The Pursuit of Pleasure: Happiness and Sexuality in Two Worldviews (Chs. 5–6)

    1. Analyze the relationship the chapter draws between worldview (e.g., atheistic vs. Christian) and capacity for happiness, citing textual evidence from Freud’s and Lewis’s own statements.
    2. Compare Lewis’s description of joy as “the central story of my life” with Psalm 16:11, evaluating how the psalm’s source of joy compares with the psychological accounts of happiness presented in the chapter.
    3. Synthesize the module’s content with Psalm 16:11 and Hebrews 13:4 to articulate a biblical account of pleasure and joy that directly engages both Freud’s and Lewis’s claims.
    4. Construct a reasoned argument for which worldview (e.g., atheistic vs. Christian) offers the more internally consistent account of the relationship between pleasure and lasting happiness, drawing evidence from both chapters.

  5. Bonds and Burdens: Love and Pain as Tests of a Worldview’s Coherence (Chs. 7–8)

    1. Compare Freud’s and Lewis’s definitions of love with their responses to suffering, identifying what common resource, if any, each man’s worldview supplies across both questions.
    2. Compare Freud’s reduction of love to sexual and desexualized categories with 1 John 4:7–8, evaluating what a biblical definition of love adds to, or challenges in, Freud’s framework.
    3. Explain the argument that the pre-conversion Lewis used against belief in a “benevolent and omnipotent spirit,” as presented in the introduction to The Problem of Pain.
    4. Compare the chapter’s treatment of suffering with 2 Corinthians 4:16-18, which highlights the contrast between physical decline and spiritual renewal, evaluating how this scriptural framing compares with Freud’s resignation and Lewis’s eventual response to pain.

  6. The Final Question: Death, Destiny, and the Verdict of a Life (Ch. 9)

    1. Contrast the atheistic and Christian expectations of death presented in the chapter (e.g., Freud’s view of death as absolute annihilation versus Lewis’s view of death as a passage to a further reality) identifying the textual evidence Nicholi offers for each.
    2. Apply Lewis’s hope of eternal life, grounded in the resurrection, to Freud’s clinical resignation in the face of terminal illness, evaluating how each man’s expectation of what follows death shaped his lived response to dying; compare both accounts with 1 Corinthians 15:54–57.
    3. Analyze how Freud’s materialist worldview, which locates the purpose of life in the reduction of tension and the pursuit of pleasure, differs from Lewis’s Christian worldview, in which purpose is derived from being created by and for God, drawing on evidence from the chapter’s treatment of mortality and meaning.
